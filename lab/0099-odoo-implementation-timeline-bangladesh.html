How long does an Odoo implementation actually take in Bangladesh?

Every proposal I have seen from a competing vendor in the last two years has quoted a timeline that a serious implementation cannot hit. This is not a rant about vendor dishonesty in the abstract. It is a phase-by-phase breakdown of what a realistic Odoo timeline looks like at three company sizes, and the specific reasons your project will run longer than any quote promises if you do not plan for them.

The Six-Week Lie

A client called me last year holding two proposals for the same manufacturing implementation: one quoted 18 weeks, one quoted 6 weeks, from two vendors who had sat through the identical discovery call and seen the identical scope document. He asked which one was right. Neither was being dishonest about the work itself. The 6-week vendor had simply scoped configuration time only, the actual clicking-and-building inside Odoo, and left out discovery, data migration, UAT, training, and hypercare entirely, either because they did not think to include it or because they knew a smaller number wins the deal.

This happens constantly in the Bangladesh Odoo market, and it happens for a structural reason, not because vendors are uniquely dishonest here. The market is price and speed sensitive, buyers routinely compare quotes primarily on cost and timeline, and a vendor who honestly quotes 5 months against a competitor's optimistic 6 weeks looks slow and expensive even when their estimate is the only one that will actually be met. The incentive runs directly against honest quoting, and most buyers do not have enough implementation experience of their own to know which number to trust.

The purpose of this piece is not to tell you Odoo implementations are slow. Most are not, if scoped correctly. It is to give you the actual phase breakdown, at three realistic company sizes, so that when a vendor hands you a number, you can tell whether it reflects a real project or a configuration estimate wearing a project's clothes.

A vendor quote is not a promise about the software. It is a promise about a project. Most timeline failures are project failures wearing a technical excuse.

The Phases, Honestly Scoped

Every legitimate Odoo implementation, regardless of company size, moves through the same seven phases. What changes with size is the duration of each phase and how much they overlap. Vendors who quote unrealistically short timelines are almost always collapsing several of these into one line item, usually "configuration," and quietly hoping the rest happens for free inside that number.

  • Discovery and requirements gathering. Structured workshops with every department, documentation of current workflows, a written Business Requirements Document. This cannot be meaningfully compressed below 2-3 weeks even for a small company, because it depends on department heads being available for interviews, not on consultant typing speed.
  • Configuration and build. The actual system setup: chart of accounts, workflows, approval rules, custom reports, any Odoo Studio or Python-level customization. This is the phase vendors are most comfortable quoting, and the one most often mistaken for the entire project.
  • Data migration. Cleaning, mapping, and importing historical customers, vendors, products, opening balances, and often years of transaction history. Almost universally underestimated, covered in detail below.
  • User Acceptance Testing. Real department staff testing real workflows with real data before go-live, not a vendor self-certifying that the system works.
  • Training. Structured, role-specific training, not a single all-hands demo three days before go-live.
  • Go-live. The cutover itself: final data sync, old system freeze, first days of live transactions under close supervision.
  • Hypercare. The intensive post-go-live support window where the vendor is on standby for the issues that only appear once real volume hits the system.

Small Company: 20-50 Users

A genuinely small Bangladesh business, a single-location trading company or a light manufacturer with limited process complexity, can realistically run this full cycle in 3 to 5 months. Discovery takes 2-3 weeks. Configuration and build run 4-6 weeks, often overlapping partially with the tail of discovery for modules that are already well understood. Data migration, assuming reasonably organized existing records, takes 2-3 weeks. UAT needs a genuine 2 weeks with department staff actually testing, not a single afternoon. Training runs 1-2 weeks, often overlapping with the end of UAT. Go-live itself is typically a single weekend or a short planned cutover window. Hypercare should run 2-3 weeks minimum.

Add these honestly and you land at roughly 14-20 weeks of calendar time, which is where the 3 to 5 month range comes from, accounting for the reality that phases rarely run perfectly sequentially and small delays compound. A vendor quoting 6-8 weeks total for this tier is quoting configuration only.

Medium Company: 50-150 Users

A medium company, typically a manufacturer or trading business with multiple departments, several warehouses, or a light multi-site footprint, needs meaningfully more discovery depth because there are more departments to interview and more edge cases in each. Discovery runs 4-6 weeks. Configuration and build, given more modules and typically some genuine customization (custom reports, approval workflows, integration with a bank or a bKash/Nagad payment gateway), runs 8-12 weeks. Data migration is heavier, often including years of transaction history across more product lines and customer accounts, and realistically needs 4-6 weeks. UAT needs 3-4 weeks across more departments and more cross-functional scenarios (a sales order flowing through inventory, manufacturing, and finance, for instance). Training needs 2-3 weeks across more roles. Hypercare should run 4-6 weeks.

Stacked with realistic overlap, this lands at 5 to 8 months. This is also the tier where I most often see vendors quote 3 months and mean it sincerely, having genuinely forgotten to size data migration and UAT against the actual data volume and department count they saw during the sales process.

Large Company: 150+ Users

At 150 or more users, typically multi-site manufacturing, export-oriented RMG groups, or companies with several sister concerns needing coordinated rollout, the timeline stops being a simple sum of phases and becomes a genuine program with its own governance. Discovery alone, done properly across multiple sites and departments, takes 6-10 weeks. Configuration and build, given the customization depth typical at this scale, runs 12-20 weeks, sometimes longer if manufacturing routing, quality control, or multi-currency export documentation are involved. Data migration at this scale, often including years of production history, BOMs, and multi-warehouse inventory, realistically needs 6-10 weeks and is the phase most likely to blow its estimate. UAT needs 4-6 weeks minimum across sites, and large companies increasingly benefit from a phased go-live, one site or one module group at a time, rather than a single big-bang cutover. Training at this scale needs 4-6 weeks given the number of roles and shifts involved. Hypercare should run 6-10 weeks, sometimes structured as a rolling hypercare that follows each site's go-live.

Honestly summed, this lands at 8 to 14 months, and I have run large Bangladesh implementations that took longer, not because of technical failure but because a phased, site-by-site rollout is genuinely the safer approach at this scale even though it extends the calendar timeline. Four Factors That Blow Every Timeline

Vendor optimism explains some overruns, but the more common cause is that both vendor and client underestimate factors on the client side that have nothing to do with the software. These four, in my experience, account for most of the gap between a quoted timeline and the actual go-live date.

Data cleanliness

Years of inconsistent spreadsheets, duplicate customer records, and unreconciled opening balances turn a planned 3-week migration into 8 weeks once someone actually opens the source files. Companies routinely underestimate this effort by 50-100% because nobody has looked closely at the data until migration starts.

Approval layers

Every configuration decision that needs sign-off from a managing director who is traveling, or from a family member who was not in the discovery workshop, adds days of waiting per decision. A company with three approval layers on every workflow decision will always run slower than one where the project sponsor can decide on the spot.

Customization scope creep

New requirements surface constantly once department heads see the system actually working and realize what else it could do. Every added report, workflow, or integration extends the build phase, and most projects have no disciplined change-request gate stopping this from happening informally, invisibly, and for free.

Client team availability

UAT and training need staff who are genuinely freed from their day jobs, not squeezing testing in between shipments. A 3-month project where the client team never had dedicated time for testing does not fail cleanly, it just quietly becomes a 6-month project with nobody having decided that on purpose.

Practical Shortcut

Before signing any timeline, ask your own team one question honestly: who is going to spend three uninterrupted hours a day testing this system for two to four weeks straight, and has their existing workload actually been reduced to make room for it? If nobody can answer that question with a name, the timeline you are about to sign is fictional regardless of what the vendor promised.

A Fast Quote Is a Vendor Signal, Not Just a Number

I want to be precise here rather than blanket-dismissive: a fast timeline is not automatically dishonest. A genuinely small, simple trading company with 10-15 users, one location, and minimal customization can honestly go live in 6-8 weeks, and I have quoted and delivered exactly that. The red flag is not speed in isolation. It is speed relative to the scope actually discussed, and speed that stands out against other vendors quoting on the identical requirements.

If three vendors bid on the same manufacturing scope and two quote 5-6 months while one quotes 6 weeks, that outlier deserves specific scrutiny before it is treated as a win. Ask exactly what is included in the 6-week number: is data migration in there. Is UAT with your own staff in there, or is UAT a single vendor-run demo. Is training scoped by role, or is it one session. Is there a hypercare period at all, or does support billing start the day after go-live. A vendor who cannot answer these specifically, or who answers vaguely with "we'll figure it out as we go," has quoted a number designed to win the deal, not a plan designed to deliver it.

This is precisely the pattern covered in the Odoo Vendor Red Flags article: an underquoted timeline behaves exactly like an underquoted price, generating change orders and scope disputes once reality catches up with the number that won the deal. Cross-reference any suspiciously fast timeline against that checklist before you sign, not after month two when the first missed milestone arrives.

How I Actually Build a Timeline

When I scope a project, I build the timeline in the order above, phase by phase, sized against the actual company profile discovered in the sales conversation, not a template number. Then I add two things most quotes leave out entirely: an explicit buffer for the approval-layer and data-cleanliness factors specific to that client, sized based on what I observed of their decision-making speed and data hygiene during the sales process itself, and a written statement of exactly what is included in each phase, so that "data migration" means a specific, agreed set of tables and record counts rather than an open-ended promise.

The uncomfortable truth is that this makes my quotes look slower than a competitor's optimistic number, every time, in every pitch. I have lost deals to a 6-week quote against my honest 4-month estimate for the same scope. I have also had a meaningful number of those same clients come back eight months later, after the 6-week vendor's project stalled at month four, asking for a recovery assessment. A realistic timeline costs you the deal sometimes. An unrealistic one costs the client the project, and eventually costs the vendor the reference.

There is also a simpler, less dramatic version of this same lesson that plays out on almost every project regardless of vendor honesty: the calendar date on a signed contract and the actual go-live date are two different things, and the gap between them is filled by whichever of the four factors above hits hardest for that specific client. I have seen a well-run, honestly-quoted medium company project finish two weeks early because the client's finance director personally drove data cleanliness with unusual discipline. I have seen an equally well-run project from the same vendor run six weeks late at a different client purely because every configuration sign-off needed the managing director's approval and he was traveling for half the project. Neither vendor did anything different. The client-side variables did.

This is why I now present timelines to clients as a range with named assumptions attached to each end of the range, rather than a single confident number. The bottom of the range assumes clean data, single-layer approvals, and a dedicated client testing team. The top of the range assumes the opposite. Most Bangladesh companies, if they are honest with themselves during the sales conversation about which end of that range their own organization resembles, can predict roughly where their project will land before it even starts. The vendors who refuse to have that conversation at all, and instead commit to a single number regardless of the client's own readiness, are the ones setting up the eventual dispute over whose fault the delay was.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a typical Odoo implementation take in Bangladesh?

For a small company with 20-50 users, a realistic Odoo implementation takes 3 to 5 months from kickoff to stable go-live including hypercare. A medium company with 50-150 users typically needs 5 to 8 months. A large company with 150 or more users, multiple sites, or manufacturing complexity typically needs 8 to 14 months. Vendors quoting 6-8 weeks for any of these tiers are almost always quoting configuration time only, not a full implementation including UAT, training, and stabilization.

Why do Odoo vendors in Bangladesh quote unrealistically short timelines?

Short timelines win deals in a price and speed-sensitive market. A vendor quoting 12 weeks looks more attractive than one quoting 20 weeks even if the 20-week estimate is the honest one. Some vendors also genuinely do not scope discovery, data migration, or UAT time into their estimate, quoting only the configuration effort. A timeline that looks too good relative to competing quotes for the same scope should be treated as a red flag worth investigating before signing.

What causes an Odoo implementation to run longer than the vendor quoted?

The most reliable causes of timeline overrun are: poor data cleanliness requiring far more migration effort than estimated, multiple internal approval layers slowing every configuration decision, customization scope creep as new requirements surface during build, and the client-side team never having genuinely dedicated time for testing and training because they are also running the business day to day. A 3-month project where the client team never had dedicated time for UAT effectively becomes a 6-month project, just an unplanned one.

What is hypercare and how long should it last?

Hypercare is the intensive support period immediately after go-live, when the vendor's team is on standby to fix issues, answer user questions, and correct configuration gaps that only appear once real transactions are flowing. For a small company, 2 to 3 weeks of hypercare is usually sufficient. For a medium company, 4 to 6 weeks. For a large or multi-site company, 6 to 10 weeks, sometimes with a phased hypercare that moves site by site.

Is a fast Odoo implementation timeline always a red flag?

Not always, but it deserves scrutiny. A genuinely small, simple business can realistically go live in 6-8 weeks. The red flag is a fast timeline quoted for a company with manufacturing complexity, multiple sites, or heavy customization requirements, especially when other vendors bidding on the identical scope are quoting two to three times longer. Cross-reference any suspiciously fast quote against the vendor evaluation criteria in the Odoo Vendor Red Flags article before signing.
