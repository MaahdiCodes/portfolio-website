The four problems that cost factories most

In Bangladeshi factories, the same four problems come up again and again, whatever the product. Each has a clear fix, and none of them starts with buying software.

01 · Inventory accuracy

Stock that matches the records

Differences between the physical store and the books are rarely theft or software bugs. They come from undocumented issues, late receipts, mixed units of measure and no counting routine.

  • Reconcile and agree an opening position
  • Clean the item master, units and locations
  • Receiving and issuing rules people can follow
  • An ABC cycle-counting routine instead of a yearly shutdown

Read: why stock never matches, cycle counting.

02 · BOM & costing

A cost per unit you can quote from

Costing sheets drift: consumption changes, wastage grows, overheads are spread by habit. The orders that look most profitable can be the ones losing money.

  • Bills of materials checked against real consumption
  • Wastage and scrap measured, not assumed
  • Labour and overhead allocated on a sensible basis
  • Standard vs actual review each month

Read: why your cost per unit is wrong.

03 · Planning & wastage

A production plan the floor follows

When the plan changes every morning, materials arrive late, lines wait, overtime climbs and wastage hides in the noise.

  • Capacity by line or machine, in hours, not hope
  • Material availability checked before an order is released
  • A 15-minute daily production meeting with a fixed agenda
  • Reorder levels for the materials that stop lines

Try: reorder point calculator. Read: dead stock.

04 · Attendance & payroll

Payroll that closes in two or three days

Most payroll delays happen before payroll: attendance arrives late, overtime is approved on paper, joiners and leavers aren't updated, and corrections pile up.

  • A cut-off calendar every department signs up to
  • Overtime approved before it's worked, in one place
  • Pre-payment checks and a month-on-month variance review
  • Salary structures that the system calculates, not Excel

Read: attendance to payroll.

How the work runs

  1. Floor walk and diagnostic (1–2 weeks). I follow materials from the gate to the store, the floor and dispatch, sample-count a set of items, trace a few orders through costing, and sit with HR through a payroll cycle. You get a short report with the evidence and a 90-day plan.
  2. Fix the foundations (4–8 weeks). Item master and BOM cleanup, the receiving and issuing rules, the counting routine, the payroll calendar. This is mostly your team's work with me alongside; it's what makes everything after it hold.
  3. Systems where they help (optional). Once the process is agreed, an ERP removes re-keying and enforces the rules. I implement Odoo (I'm an Odoo Certified Functional Consultant) or work with the system you already have.
  4. Hand over and measure. Three or four numbers prove it's working: inventory record accuracy, cost variance, plan adherence, payroll close days. Your managers own them; I check in monthly until they're stable.

Frequently asked questions

Do we need an ERP to fix inventory problems in our factory?

Not always, and never first. Most stock differences come from process gaps: goods issued without a document, receipts posted late, units of measure that differ between purchase and store, and no counting routine. Those must be fixed whether you use Excel or an ERP. An ERP helps once the process is agreed, because it enforces it and removes re-keying.

How long does it take to get stock accurate?

For a single factory, a reconciliation and the start of a cycle-counting routine usually take four to eight weeks. Accuracy on high-value items improves first; the long tail of low-value items takes a few more months of regular counting.

Can you work with our existing ERP or software?

Yes. Most of the work is process, data and discipline, which applies to any system. Where configuration has to change, I work with your vendor or in-house team.

Which industries do you work with?

Mainly textile and garments, light engineering and bicycles, plastics and packaging, food processing and other discrete or batch manufacturers, plus importers and distributors with large stores. See the industry guides.
