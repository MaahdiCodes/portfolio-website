The item

Lead time runs from the day you raise the order to the day goods are usable in store, including approval, payment, shipping, clearing and inspection. If a supplier is "usually up to 4–6 days late", a variation of 2–3 days is a fair start.

%

Ordering cost covers the staff time, approvals, LC or bank charges, transport and inspection for one order. Holding cost covers the money tied up, storage, insurance, damage and obsolescence; 18–25% of the item's value per year is a reasonable starting range.

    How the numbers are worked out

    Safety stock is the buffer that covers the two things that go wrong during a lead time: you use more than average, or the supplier is late. The calculator combines both: safety stock = z × √(lead time × usage variation² + daily usage² × lead-time variation²), where z comes from the service level (1.645 for 95%).

    Reorder point = average usage during the lead time + safety stock. When the stock you actually have, plus what is already on order, falls to this level, raise the next order.

    Order quantity is the economic order quantity, √(2 × annual usage × ordering cost ÷ holding cost per unit). It balances the cost of ordering often against the cost of holding a lot. If the supplier's minimum order is higher, the calculator uses the minimum.

    If you choose a usage pattern instead of a standard deviation, the calculator assumes the daily variation is 25%, 50% or 90% of average usage. For a better figure, take 3–6 months of daily issues from your store records and use Excel's STDEV.S on them.

    Before you trust any reorder point

    • The stock figure must be right. A reorder point compared with a wrong on-hand quantity orders too late or too early. If your physical counts don't match the system, fix that first: why stock never matches the system and cycle counting.
    • Count what's on order. Compare the reorder point with on-hand plus open purchase orders, or you will order the same goods twice.
    • Use real lead times. Measure them from your own purchase orders and receipts, not from the supplier's quotation.
    • Review quarterly. Usage and lead times change with seasons, buyers and suppliers. A reorder point set once and never reviewed becomes either a stock-out or a pile of dead stock.
    • Not every item needs one. Make-to-order items, one-off customer materials and very slow movers are better ordered against actual demand.
