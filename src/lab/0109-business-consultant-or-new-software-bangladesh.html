The Expensive Mistake

The pattern is common in Bangladeshi companies that have grown fast. Sales doubled, a second factory or warehouse opened, the team grew from 40 to 300, and the way of working that suited a small company stopped holding together. Reports are late, stock is wrong, purchases need the owner's signature, and every month-end is a scramble.

The owner concludes the company has outgrown its tools and buys an ERP. Six months later the ERP is live, and the same problems are still there, now inside a system that cost lakhs of taka and that the team works around. The software wasn't the cause, so it couldn't be the cure. Why ERP fails in Bangladesh describes how often this happens.

Software is excellent at enforcing an agreed way of working and removing re-keying. It is poor at deciding what the way of working should be, at getting departments to agree, and at making people follow a rule they don't accept. Those are management and process problems, and they need to be solved first, or at least at the same time.

The 15 Questions

Answer each with a plain yes or no for your company today. Be honest; nobody else will see it.

Process: is the way of working agreed?

  1. If you asked three people how a customer order becomes a delivery and an invoice, would they describe the same steps?
  2. Is it clear who approves a purchase, a price change and customer credit, and up to what amount, without asking the owner?
  3. Does every movement of goods (receipt, issue, transfer, return, scrap) happen with a document, every time?
  4. Do month-end figures arrive within ten days, without someone rebuilding them by hand?
  5. When something goes wrong, can you usually find out which step failed and who owned it?

People: will the team work the new way?

  1. Do department heads agree on the problem, or does each blame another department?
  2. Is there someone other than the owner with the time and authority to lead a change for six months?
  3. Have past changes (a new form, a new rule, a new report) actually stuck?
  4. Are the people who would use a new system comfortable with the current tools, such as Excel or email?
  5. Will the owner let a rule apply to everyone, including senior staff and family members in the business?

Data and systems: is the problem really the tool?

  1. Is your item, customer and supplier data reasonably clean: one code per thing, consistent units?
  2. Is the main pain re-entering the same information in several places?
  3. Has volume grown so much that spreadsheets are slow, break, or can be edited by too many people at once?
  4. Do you need information across several sites or companies that today can only be combined by hand?
  5. Is there a specific report or control you need that your current tools genuinely can't produce?

Reading Your Answers

PatternWhat it means
Mostly no to questions 1–10, mostly yes to 11–15Real system limits on top of real process problems. Fix the process first, or alongside the system. A system bought now will fail for process reasons.
Mostly no to 1–10, mostly no to 11–15The problem is process and management, not software. A consultant or an internal improvement lead will get you further than any purchase.
Mostly yes to 1–10, mostly yes to 11–15You're ready for software. The process is agreed and the tools genuinely can't keep up. Choose and implement a system, carefully.
Mostly yes to 1–10, mostly no to 11–15You're in good shape. Look for specific improvements rather than a big project.

Pay special attention to questions 3, 6 and 10. A "no" to any of them is a warning sign whatever else you answered: goods moving without documents, departments that don't agree on the problem, or rules that don't apply to everyone will defeat any system.

Software enforces a way of working. It can't choose one for you, and it can't make people agree to it.

What to Do Next

If it's mostly process

  • Pick the one problem that costs most: usually stock accuracy, month-end reporting or payroll.
  • Map how that work really happens today, step by step, with the people who do it. As-is to to-be process mapping shows how.
  • Agree the new way of working, who owns each step, and the document used at each hand-over.
  • Write it as a one-page procedure and enforce it for three months. Measure one number before and after.
  • Then move to the next problem. After two or three, revisit the software question.

If it's mostly system

  • Write down the requirements in business language before talking to any vendor. The requirements template helps.
  • Check readiness honestly with the ERP readiness assessment.
  • Get a realistic budget range from the cost estimator before vendor meetings.
  • Plan time for data cleanup and training, which cost more than most companies expect.

If it's both

Do them in the right order: agree the process for each area before it's configured, and let the implementation be the moment the new way of working starts. That's slower at the beginning and much faster overall.

Choosing a Consultant in Bangladesh

"Business consultant" covers very different services in Bangladesh: strategy firms, accounting firms with advisory arms, donor-funded programmes, software vendors who call their sales team consultants, and independent specialists. Ask any of them:

  • What will I have at the end? A report, a changed process, a trained team, a working system? If the answer is only a report, ask who will implement it.
  • Who will actually do the work? The person in the meeting, or a junior team?
  • Have you done this in a company like mine? Same industry, similar size, similar problems. Ask for a reference you can call.
  • How are you paid? Fixed fee, time, or commission on software you recommend? Commission isn't wrong, but you should know.
  • What don't you do? A good consultant is clear about their limits. Tax and statutory audit, for example, belong with accountants.
  • How will we know it worked? Agree two or three numbers up front.

What a Good Diagnostic Gives You

Whoever you work with, the first step should be a short, fixed-price diagnostic rather than a long open-ended project. At the end of one or two weeks you should have:

  1. A one-page summary for the owner: what's working, what isn't, and why, in plain language.
  2. Evidence for each finding: documents traced, counts done, people interviewed.
  3. A short list of priorities ranked by cost to the business, not by what's easy.
  4. A 90-day plan with owners and rough costs, and a clear view of whether software is part of it.
  5. Two or three numbers to measure progress.

A diagnostic that ends with "you need a big project with us" and no evidence is a sales pitch.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my business needs a consultant?

When the same problem keeps coming back despite people working hard, when departments disagree on the cause, or when everything waits for the owner. Those are signs of a process or management problem that an outside view can usually untangle faster than the team can from inside.

Is it better to hire a consultant or a full-time manager?

For a one-off change, a consultant is usually faster and cheaper, and brings experience from other companies. For ongoing management, hire. Many companies use a consultant to design and set up the change, and hire or promote the person who will run it afterwards.

Should we buy an ERP first and fix processes during implementation?

Only if the implementation partner will genuinely spend time on process design, and your managers have time to decide. In practice, most implementations configure the software around current habits. Agreeing the key processes first makes the implementation faster and the result more useful.

What does a business consultant cost in Bangladesh?

It varies widely with the firm and the scope. Ask for a fixed price for a defined first step, such as a diagnostic, rather than an open-ended daily rate. That lets you judge the quality of the work before committing to more.
