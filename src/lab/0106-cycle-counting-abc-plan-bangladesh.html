Why the Yearly Count Fails

The full stock take at the end of June or December is a ritual in most Bangladeshi companies. Production stops, everyone counts, the numbers go to accounts, and a difference is written off. It fails for three reasons:

  • It's too late. A difference found in June may have started in August. Nobody can trace ten months of transactions, so the cause is never found and the same problem continues.
  • It's done badly. Hundreds of people count thousands of items under time pressure, many of them unfamiliar with the materials. Counting errors are common, and there's no time to recount.
  • It treats every item the same. A drum of imported dye and a box of cable ties get the same attention, once a year.

Cycle counting fixes all three: small daily counts done by trained people, high-value items counted often, and every difference investigated within days. Before you start, make sure the process gaps that create differences are being closed; why stock never matches the system covers that. Counting a store where goods are still issued without documents just measures the problem more often.

Step 1: Classify Items A, B, C

ABC classification ranks items by annual consumption value: how much of the item you use in a year multiplied by its unit cost. Not stock value, and not quantity.

  1. Export twelve months of issues (or consumption) per item, and the current unit cost.
  2. Multiply to get annual consumption value, and sort from highest to lowest.
  3. Add a running total as a percentage of the grand total.
  4. Items making up the first ~80% of value are A, the next ~15% are B, the last ~5% are C.

The pattern is almost always similar: a small share of items, often 10–20%, carries most of the value. In a garment factory that's usually fabric, some trims and imported chemicals; in light engineering, steel, purchased components and paint; in plastics, resin and masterbatch.

Then adjust by judgement. Move an item up a class if it stops production when it runs out (a cheap spare part for a critical machine), if it's easy to steal, or if it has a history of large differences. Move it down if it's high value but barely moves and is stored sealed.

Step 2: Set Frequency and Workload

A common starting point:

ClassShare of items (typical)Count frequency
A10–20%Monthly (12 times a year)
B20–30%Quarterly (4 times a year)
C50–70%Once or twice a year

Now check the workload is realistic. Take an example store with 2,000 active items: 200 A items counted 12 times is 2,400 counts; 500 B items counted 4 times is 2,000; 1,300 C items counted once is 1,300. That's 5,700 counts a year. Over 50 working weeks of six days, it's about 19 items a day, which one trained person can do in a couple of hours, alongside other work.

If the number is too high, reduce C frequency or count C items by location (one rack a week) rather than by item. If it's comfortably low, count A items more often.

Step 3: Agree Tolerances

A tolerance says how big a difference can be before it counts as an error. Without it, every count of a material measured by weight or length will "fail", and people stop taking the results seriously. Starting points:

  • A items: ±0.5% to ±1% of quantity, or a small fixed value.
  • B items: ±2%.
  • C items: ±5%.
  • Counted by piece (motors, bearings, finished cartons): zero tolerance. A missing piece is a missing piece.

Write the tolerances down and have the factory head and accounts agree to them. They are a management decision, not something the store keeper should choose.

Step 4: The Daily Routine

The routine matters more than the plan. Keep it the same every day:

  1. Generate the list. Each morning, the list of items due for counting today, with their locations. Not the system quantity.
  2. Count at a fixed time. Early, before the first issue of the day, or at a time when that area is quiet. Post every document for those items up to that time before comparing.
  3. Count blind. The counter writes down what they find without seeing what the system says. This one rule removes most "confirmation counting".
  4. Check every location. Many differences are simply stock in a second location: the floor, the inspection area, a sister store.
  5. Compare and flag. Anything outside tolerance is flagged for a recount the same day by a different person.

Who counts? Ideally someone who doesn't issue the material: an inventory controller, an internal auditor, or a store person from a different section. In a small company where that's impossible, rotate counters and keep approvals with someone outside the store.

Step 5: Investigate, Then Approve

A recount that confirms a difference starts an investigation, not an adjustment. Look at the transactions since the last count: receipts, issues, returns, transfers, scrap. Most differences have an explanation within a day or two of digging, and the explanation is what tells you which process to fix.

Then adjust, with approval levels set by value. For example: the store in-charge approves small differences, the factory head approves medium ones, and the managing director or finance head approves anything large. Record the cause for every adjustment, using a short fixed list of causes so you can count them later.

Keep the roles separate

The person who counts shouldn't approve the adjustment, and the person who approves shouldn't post it. In a 30-person company this can feel bureaucratic, but it's the control that makes the numbers believable to an owner, a bank or a buyer's auditor.

Measuring Progress

Track three numbers monthly, by class:

  • Inventory record accuracy: items counted within tolerance ÷ items counted.
  • Absolute value of differences: the sum of all differences ignoring sign, before adjustment.
  • Top causes: how many differences each cause explained.

Expect A-item accuracy to rise first, because those items get the most attention. When the causes list stops changing month to month, the process fixes are working and you can reduce how often you count.

Most ERPs, including Odoo, can schedule counts by location or product and record counted quantities against system quantities, which removes the spreadsheet work. The routine and the approvals are the same either way.

Frequently asked questions

Do we still need a year-end stock take for the auditors?

That depends on what your auditors require, so ask them. Many will accept a well-documented cycle-counting programme with good accuracy results as a reason to reduce the scope of the full count, or to replace it. Even where a year-end count is still required, it becomes faster and produces fewer surprises once cycle counting is in place.

How many items should we count per day?

Work it out from your item counts and frequencies, then check one person can do it in two to three hours. For a store with around 2,000 active items, about 20 a day is typical. Start smaller if needed; regular small counts beat an ambitious plan that stops after three weeks.

Should we count by item or by location?

Count A and B items by item, checking every location they're stored in. Count low-value C items by location, one rack or bay at a time, which is quicker and also finds items in the wrong place.

What if the counts keep finding differences?

Then the counting is doing its job. Look at the causes column: if the same two or three causes keep appearing, they point to process gaps that need fixing at their source, usually in issuing, receiving or units of measure.
