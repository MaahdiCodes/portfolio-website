Slow, Non-Moving and Dead

The words get used loosely. Agree definitions in your company, based on the date each item was last issued or sold, not the date it was bought:

CategoryA common definitionAction
Slow-movingSome movement, but stock covers far more than normal, e.g. more than 6 months of usageStop reordering; review the reorder level
Non-movingNo issue or sale for 6–12 monthsFind a use or a buyer; decide within a quarter
DeadNo movement for over 12 months, or no foreseeable demand at allDecide now: use, return, sell, transfer or scrap

Adjust the thresholds to your business. Seasonal materials, such as winter fabrics, and slow-moving critical spares, such as a gearbox for the main machine, need their own rules. A spare that protects a production line isn't dead stock even if it hasn't moved in two years; it's insurance, and should be listed as such.

How It Builds Up

Dead stock is created by decisions that each made sense at the time:

  • Buyer- or order-specific material. Fabric, trims, labels and packaging bought for a particular buyer or style. When the order is cut, cancelled or changed, the surplus has no other use. This is the biggest source in garment and textile factories.
  • Supplier minimum orders. You needed 300 kg; the supplier's minimum was 1,000 kg. The other 700 kg waits for a next order that may never come.
  • Design and engineering changes. A component is replaced by a new version, and the old stock is never used up first.
  • Reorder levels nobody reviews. A reorder level set when an item sold well keeps triggering orders after demand has fallen.
  • Duplicate codes. Stock under an old or duplicate code is invisible to planners, so the same material is bought again under the active code.
  • "Just in case" buying. Price rises, currency worries or supply scares lead to buying ahead, and some of it is never needed.

Finding It: the Ageing Report

The tool is an ageing report: every item with stock, its quantity and value, and the number of days since it last moved, grouped into bands such as 0–90, 91–180, 181–365 and over 365 days. Any ERP can produce it; in Excel you need the last issue date per item from your stock ledger.

  1. Produce the report and sort by value within each age band.
  2. Physically check the top items in the older bands: is the stock really there, in what condition, and is it still usable?
  3. Ask planning, sales and production about each one: is there a real, dated use for it?
  4. Record a decision and an owner for each item over your threshold.

Check the stock figure before acting on it. If physical stock and records don't match, the ageing report will be wrong too, and some "dead" stock won't exist. Why stock never matches the system covers that first step.

Valuing It Honestly

Most companies carry dead stock in the books at what it cost. If it can only be sold as stock lot or scrap, its real value is much lower, and the balance sheet overstates the business's assets. That affects how the owner, the bank and any investor see the company.

For operational decisions, estimate a realistic recoverable value for each item: what it would fetch if sold today, minus the cost of selling it, or its value if used internally as a substitute. The difference between book value and recoverable value is the loss that has already happened; deciding quickly stops it growing through further damage, storage cost and obsolescence. How and when to write stock down in the accounts is a decision to make with your accountant.

Dead stock isn't a loss you take when you scrap it. The loss happened when it stopped being useful. Waiting only adds storage cost.

Clearing It: Five Options

Work through these in order, because the first ones recover the most value:

  1. Use it internally. Can it substitute for a current material, in a sample, a lower-specification product, a local-market order, or as packaging or maintenance stock? Planning should check dead stock before raising a new purchase of anything similar.
  2. Return it to the supplier. Some suppliers take back unused, undamaged standard material, often with a restocking charge. Worth asking for anything recent and in its original packing.
  3. Transfer it within the group. A sister concern may use the material. Record it as a proper transfer at an agreed value.
  4. Sell it. As surplus to other manufacturers, or to stock-lot buyers. Bangladesh has an active market for surplus fabric, yarn and accessories. Get several offers, and remove buyer labels or branding where required by your buyer agreements.
  5. Scrap it. When nothing else works, scrap it deliberately: approved, documented, removed from stock, and sold as scrap where possible. Holding it longer only costs space and attention.

Every disposal should have an approval at the right level, a document, and a stock entry. Disposals that bypass the system are how scrap sales turn into new stock differences.

Stopping New Dead Stock

Clearing old stock is a one-off. Preventing new dead stock is a set of habits:

  • A monthly ageing review. Fifteen minutes in the operations meeting: what moved into the over-180-days band this month, and who owns it.
  • Order-specific purchasing rules. Material bought for a specific order is linked to that order, and when the order changes, the surplus is flagged the same week, while it can still be returned or used.
  • Reorder levels reviewed quarterly. Set from real usage and lead times, and recalculated when demand changes. The reorder point calculator does the arithmetic, and reordering rules vs make-to-order covers when not to use them.
  • An approval for large cover. Any purchase that takes stock above, say, six months of usage needs a second approval and a reason.
  • Use-old-first on changes. When a design or specification changes, plan to use up old stock first, or decide on the spot what happens to it.
  • One person creates item codes. Fewer duplicates, so planners can see what you already have.

Frequently asked questions

When does slow-moving stock become dead stock?

A common rule is no movement for more than 12 months, or no foreseeable demand at all. Agree the definition in your company and apply it from the last issue or sale date, with separate rules for seasonal materials and critical spares.

How do we find dead stock in our ERP?

Run a stock ageing report based on the last movement date, grouped into age bands and sorted by value. Most ERPs, including Odoo, can produce one; in Excel you need the last issue date per item from the stock ledger. Check the stock physically before acting on it.

Should we sell dead stock at a loss?

Usually yes, once internal use and returns have been ruled out. The loss already happened when the stock stopped being useful; selling recovers some cash and stops storage and damage costs. Discuss the accounting treatment with your accountant.

How do garment factories handle leftover fabric and trims?

Link order-specific materials to their order, flag surplus as soon as an order changes, use it for samples or other orders where the buyer allows, and sell the rest to surplus or stock-lot buyers, removing buyer branding where agreements require it.
