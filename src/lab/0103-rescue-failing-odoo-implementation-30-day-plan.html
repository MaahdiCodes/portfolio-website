Is It Actually Failing?

Every implementation feels like it is failing at some point. The weeks around go-live are noisy: users complain, reports look strange, and someone senior asks why the company paid for this. That noise is normal and usually settles during hypercare. A failing implementation is different. It shows up as a steady loss of trust that doesn't recover once the go-live noise dies down.

The test I use is about trust, not features. Look for these four signs three or more months after go-live:

  • Parallel spreadsheets. Store, accounts or production keep their own Excel "to be safe", and that sheet, not the system, is what people believe.
  • Stock that doesn't match. A physical count disagrees with the system by more than a rounding error, or nobody has dared to count since go-live.
  • A slower month-end. Closing the books takes longer than it did before the ERP, because accounts correct entries by hand before anything can be reported.
  • Compliance outside the system. Mushak 6.3, the VAT return or payroll is still prepared outside Odoo from exported data.

If two or more of these are true, the implementation is failing, even if every module is technically "live". If you want a quicker read, the rescue self-check weighs twelve warning signs in two minutes.

Week 1: Stop the Bleeding

The first week isn't for fixing anything. It's for making sure things stop getting worse, and for securing what you'll need later.

Secure access and ownership

Before any conversation about blame or contracts, make sure the company holds administrator access to the production database, a recent backup it can restore, the source code of every custom module (in a repository the company controls), and the hosting credentials. If a partner relationship is souring, this is the moment it matters most. Getting these after a dispute starts is far harder. Backup and restore covers the technical side.

Freeze changes

Failing projects often have several people changing configuration at once, each fixing their own symptom. Freeze everything except fixes that block daily operations, and log every change made from now on: who, what, why. You can't diagnose a system that keeps moving.

Protect the critical outputs

Identify the three or four outputs the business cannot miss this month: salaries, customer invoices with valid Mushak 6.3, the VAT return, shipments against open LCs. Make sure each has a working path, even a temporary manual one, and a named owner. A rescue that causes a missed payroll loses the owner's support in a day.

Practical Shortcut

Write a one-page "what we know" note at the end of week one: who has access to what, what's frozen, which critical outputs are protected and by whom. It's the first document the owner and any new consultant will ask for, and writing it often shows how much nobody knows.

Week 2: Diagnose, Don't Blame

The diagnosis has to look at the business, not only the software. I trace the four core flows end to end, order-to-cash, procure-to-pay, plan-to-produce and record-to-report, by sitting with the people who run them, not by reading the configuration.

Follow real transactions

Pick ten recent real documents in each flow, a sales order, a purchase, a production order, and follow each one through the system. Where does it leave Odoo for paper, Excel or WhatsApp? Where does someone re-enter data that already exists? Each exit point is a symptom, and the pattern of exits tells you the cause.

Test the data

Count stock for a sample of high-value items and compare it with the system. Check for duplicate products, customers and vendors, and for products set up in the wrong unit of measure. Reconcile one bank account and one large customer's balance. In most failing implementations, the data is where trust was lost first. See the data migration checklist for what should have been checked at go-live.

List every customisation

For each custom module, write down what it does, who asked for it, whether standard Odoo now covers it, and whether it touches core code. This list usually explains why upgrades are stuck and why fixing one thing breaks another.

Talk to the users

Interview two or three users per department, without their managers in the room. Ask what they do outside the system and why. Users rarely reject an ERP for no reason. They reject it because it made their job harder, or because they were never shown how it makes the job easier.

A failing implementation is rarely one big mistake. It is usually twenty small ones that all point back to the same three or four causes.

Week 3: Rescue or Restart

With a diagnosis in hand, the decision is evidence-based. Most implementations should be rescued: a restart throws away working configuration, migrated data and everything users have already learned, and it usually carries the same process and data problems straight into the new system.

Partner change is a separate question. If the diagnosis shows the partner can't deliver, plan the exit carefully. Switching Odoo partners mid-implementation covers the handover. If the causes were shared, unsigned scope or process owners who were never available, a new partner will hit the same wall.

Week 4: The Recovery Plan

The plan should fit on two pages and be approved by the owner, not just the IT lead. It needs:

  1. A prioritised fix list. Ranked by business impact, not technical difficulty. Stock accuracy and month-end close usually come first because they rebuild trust fastest.
  2. Named owners. One business owner per flow who signs off fixes, plus a data owner for each master-data type: products, customers, vendors, chart of accounts.
  3. A clean cut-off date. The date from which the system becomes the single source of truth again and parallel spreadsheets are retired.
  4. Re-training by role. Short, role-specific sessions on the corrected flows, not another full demo. The training plan template works for this.
  5. Success measures. Pick three numbers and measure them before and after: days to close the month, stock count variance, and how many hours VAT filing takes. Those numbers are what convinces the owner, and later your next client.

Winning the Users Back

The technical fixes are the easy half. Users who were burned once will keep their spreadsheets until the system proves itself, and that proof takes time: usually one or two clean month-end closes in a row, and a stock count that matches.

What helps: tell users what changed and why, in their language, at their desk. Fix their top complaint first, even if it isn't technically the most important, because it shows the rescue is about them. Appoint a super-user in each department who gets answers fast. And retire the parallel spreadsheets on the cut-off date, deliberately and visibly, with management behind it. As long as the spreadsheet is allowed, it will win.

Mistakes That Make It Worse

  • Firing the partner before securing the code and database. You lose the evidence and the access in one move.
  • Adding modules to fix adoption. A new module on a broken foundation adds a new failure point.
  • More customisation. If users reject a standard flow, find out why before writing code around it. Often the process needs to change, not the software.
  • Leaving the owner out. Rescues that are run by the IT team alone stall the first time a department head refuses to change how they work.
  • Declaring victory on go-live of the fixes. Success is the second clean month-end, not the deployment date.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know if my Odoo implementation is failing?

The clearest signs are about trust, not features: staff keep parallel Excel sheets, system stock doesn't match a physical count, month-end close takes longer than before the ERP, and the VAT return is prepared outside the system. If two or more of these are true several months after go-live, the implementation is failing even if every module is technically live.

Should we fire our Odoo partner if the project is failing?

Not in the first week. Before changing partners, secure access to the database, source code and documentation, and find out what is actually broken. Many failures are shared between partner and client, such as unsigned scope or unavailable process owners, and those causes follow you to the next partner. Decide after the diagnosis, not before it.

How long does an Odoo rescue take?

The triage and diagnosis described here takes about 30 days. The fix work that follows is usually 4 to 8 weeks for a single company with a handful of core modules, longer for multi-company or multi-site groups. Rebuilding user trust takes longer than the technical fixes, typically one or two clean month-end closes in a row.

Is it cheaper to rescue Odoo or to switch to another ERP?

Usually rescuing is cheaper, because a switch throws away working configuration, migrated data and everything users have already learned, and it carries over the same process and data problems that caused the failure. Switching makes sense only when the current system's foundation is broken, for example core code modified so heavily that it cannot be upgraded.
